```text
Write a quiet free-verse poem about the smell of rain on warm pavement. It is for me, for its own sake, not for marketing or publication. Use plain language. Keep it under 120 words. No rhyme scheme, and no explanatory note after the poem.

Start from the moment itself, not from ideas about rain. Before drafting, list 15–20 concrete particulars of how it happens: first drops darkening dry concrete one spot at a time, warmth lifting off the ground, the smell arriving before the rain gets heavy and thinning once everything is soaked, what else is there (a parked car, chalk, an open window, someone standing in a doorway). Keep only details you can picture exactly.

Borrow a perfumer's method: a perfumer describes a scent by what it resembles and by how it changes over time, from the first breath to what it settles into to what lingers. Let that arc shape the poem, so it moves the way the smell moves instead of summarizing rain. (Assumption: the smell is the subject; the rain is its occasion.)

Keep it quiet: short lines, space on the page, soft sounds. Read each line aloud and cut any line that explains, pushes, or names a feeling the details already carry. Avoid stock rain images (tears, cleansing, renewal) and technical words like "petrichor."

Write three short drafts that take different routes into the moment, choose the one closest to the actual experience, and revise it. Show only the final poem.

Judge: before replying, count the poem's words, title included, with `wc -w` or an equivalent count, and confirm the total is under 120. Check the line endings and confirm no rhyme pattern. Reply with the poem alone, with nothing after it. Whether it brings the smell back is my call when I read it. That stays unverified until then.
```
