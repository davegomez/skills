```text
Draft a weekly report about recurring checkout failures so our team can choose what to fix.

Assumption to confirm: the report should distinguish “fix next” candidates from issues that need more evidence before a fix can be chosen. Recommend decisions; do not implement them.

Boundaries
- Inspect only the redacted incident notes I provide.
- Do not access production, change checkout code, create accounts, send messages, or change incident-response policy.
- Do not supplement the notes with outside sources or infer details hidden by redaction.
- Any suggestion to move beyond the report must be a separate proposal requiring my approval. This task grants no authority to execute it.

First checks
- Check whether I have supplied the notes and identified the reporting week. Request missing inputs rather than inventing them.
- Ask whether I can provide qualified review of the report’s checkout/incident reasoning independently of its author. Do not contact or recruit anyone. Until that review occurs, label the recommendations “not independently validated.”
- Identify gaps in the supplied notes that limit conclusions, but do not seek additional sources.

Build the evidence
- Assign stable references to the supplied notes so every material finding can be traced to its supporting passages.
- Reconstruct distinct incidents and their observed sequences. Separate repeated mentions of one incident from separate failures; flag ambiguous duplicates rather than silently counting them.
- Group recurring failure signatures without assuming similar symptoms have the same cause. Separate observed facts, hypotheses, and unknowns.
- Test the premise that each recurring group represents one fixable problem: look within the notes for differences in checkout stage, conditions, timing, and outcomes that would argue for splitting the group. Do not claim a root cause or a fix’s effectiveness without supporting evidence.
- Count only what the notes support. Label counts as documented incidents within these notes, not production incidence rates. Missing denominators, coverage, or impact measurements remain explicit unknowns.

Make the report decision-ready
Keep it concise, with:
1. A weekly summary of the strongest supported patterns and important limits.
2. An evidence-linked table: failure signature; documented incidents and counting uncertainty; observed customer impact; recurrence evidence; causal confidence; proposed fix or investigation decision.
3. A short ordered recommendation list. Use decision analysis to compare documented impact, recurrence, and evidential confidence without inventing numerical scores. Explain why each candidate outranks the next.
4. A robustness check: identify which rankings remain sensible despite missing information, which could reverse, and the specific missing fact that would change the decision. Red-team the leading recommendation with the strongest contrary evidence in the notes. If the evidence cannot support a winner, say so.
5. Limitations and validation status.

Do not manufacture a fix recommendation where the notes support only a failure description. Keep proposed findings visibly separate from observed evidence.

With my approval only: pursue any follow-up beyond this report, described in a separate “Proposal—approval required” section with its purpose, needed evidence, access, and risks. Execute none of it; the prohibitions above remain in force.

Judge and observable pass condition
I independently review the report against the supplied notes, provided I can assess the checkout/incident reasoning. Pass requires: every material factual claim traces to supporting notes; duplicate ambiguity and missing coverage are disclosed; causal language matches the evidence; and each priority is justified over its alternatives or explicitly left unresolved. A material contradiction or unsupported priority fails review and requires revision. Your own checklist is preparation, not independent validation. Include the actual review outcome only after review occurs; otherwise mark it pending.
```
