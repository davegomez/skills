```text
Critique this sentence for a manager who wants to be candid without humiliating a colleague: "Your presentation was a mess and everyone was confused."
Give one revised sentence and a short explanation. Preserve the message that the presentation was difficult to follow. Do not invent what happened in the meeting, diagnose the colleague, or suggest a performance-management process.

Goal (assumption: the manager wants the colleague to hear the point and present more clearly next time, not just to soften the wording): write a sentence the colleague can accept without feeling publicly shamed and can act on, while the difficulty-following message stays unmistakable.

Method:
- Take the original apart clause by clause before rewriting. Sort each part into one of three kinds, following the observation-versus-evaluation split from Nonviolent Communication:
  - an observation the manager can stand behind;
  - an evaluative label, such as "a mess", which judges the work and gives no information;
  - a claim about other people's minds, such as "everyone was confused", which the manager cannot verify and which turns private feedback into a report of public failure.
  Keep the observation. Drop or reframe the rest.
- Use the Situation-Behavior-Impact feedback model only as far as the facts allow. The impact is "difficult to follow." Do not supply a situation or behavior detail the manager did not give.
- Speak in the manager's own voice only if the original supports it. "I found it hard to follow" assumes the manager was among the confused. If that assumption does not hold, keep the claim about the presentation itself, for example "the presentation was hard to follow," and note the choice in the explanation.
- Apply politeness theory (Brown and Levinson's face-threatening acts) to the message: it should be on-record and direct, with redress. Lower the face threat by aiming the critique at the presentation rather than the person, and optionally by offering to talk it through. Do not hedge until the point disappears.
- Red-team the draft from two directions before finalizing:
  - Read it as the colleague, on a bad day. Does any word still sting, imply an audience judged them, or label them?
  - Read it as a blunt manager. Would the colleague walk away unsure that the presentation was hard to follow? If so, the sentence is too soft.
  Revise until it passes both readings.

Constraints, unchanged:
- One revised sentence and a short explanation, nothing more.
- Preserve the message that the presentation was difficult to follow.
- Invent no meeting details.
- No diagnosis of the colleague.
- No performance-management process. An offer to discuss is a conversation, not a process; do not frame it as one.

With my approval only: show the original and revised sentences to one trusted peer who was not in the meeting, as an independent reader. This shares feedback about a named colleague.

Judge: the manager (independent of the author), or the approved peer, scores the revised sentence against this rubric:
1. The colleague would clearly understand that the presentation was difficult to follow.
2. It contains no meeting details beyond the original, no claim about what "everyone" thought, and no diagnosis of the colleague.
3. It contains no performance-management language.
4. A reader in the colleague's position would not feel humiliated.
Pass condition: all 4 items are yes. The deliverable has no room for a review report, so this validation stays pending until the manager or peer applies the rubric. The model's own read of the rubric is a revision aid, not the verdict.
```
