```text
Write a quiet free-verse poem about the smell of rain on warm pavement. It is for my own enjoyment, not for marketing or publication.

Requirements: plain language, under 120 words, no rhyme scheme, and nothing after the poem: no explanatory note, title commentary, or word count.

How to work:
- Start from raw sensory material, not the idea of rain. Before drafting, privately list 10–15 concrete particulars of the moment: the first fat drops darkening the concrete one spot at a time, heat still rising from the ground, dust lifting, the smell arriving before the sound, how long it lasts, where a person might be standing. Draw the poem from the strongest few and discard the rest.
- Borrow a perfumer's method for describing smell. Perfumers track a scent in time: what hits first, what it becomes, what lingers. They also name smells by what they are like rather than by abstract adjectives. Let the poem follow the smell's arc from first breath to fade, so its shape comes from the smell rather than from a stated theme.
- Keep it quiet: short lines, ordinary words, no exclamation, no explaining what the smell means. Prefer one exact detail over a general phrase. Avoid "petrichor" and other words a person wouldn't say aloud on a porch.
- Revise like a working poet. Read the draft line by line, cut every word that does not earn its place, and replace stock phrases (e.g. "dancing raindrops", "cleansing rain", "memories flooding back") with something observed.

Judge, run privately before delivering:
- Count the words with a tool (for example `wc -w`). Pass: fewer than 120.
- List the last word of each line and check for end-rhyme. Pass: no repeating rhyme pattern. An occasional accidental near-rhyme is fine.
- Pass: the output is the poem alone, with no text after it.
Whether the poem is quiet and plain is for me to judge as its reader. Treat your own sense of that as a revision guide, not as a pass.
```
