Critique this sentence for a manager who wants to be candid without humiliating a colleague: "Your presentation was a mess and everyone was confused."

Goal (assumption: the manager wants the feedback heard, and wants the colleague's next presentation to be easier to follow, not just a gentler sentence): write one sentence the colleague can hear without losing face and act on, while it plainly says the presentation was difficult to follow.

Critique from the sentence's own words, not from a general impression:
- "a mess" is a global verdict on the work. It names no quality the colleague can change.
- "everyone was confused" speaks for people who did not speak, and it implies public failure. That implication causes most of the humiliation.
- "Your ... mess" ties the verdict to the person rather than to the presentation's clarity.

Revise using these methods, and say what each does here:
- Situation-Behavior-Impact feedback: anchor the sentence to the presentation and its effect (hard to follow), not to a character judgment.
- First-person observation: state what the manager experienced ("I found it hard to follow"), which the original's "everyone" already includes. Do not add any new claim about the audience or the meeting.
- Politeness theory (Brown & Levinson): deliver the face-threatening point on the record, stated directly, with redress that eases the sting. Use a forward-looking or collaborative clause as the redress, not hedges that blur the point.
- Forward step: if it fits in the same sentence, end with an open invitation to talk about making the next one easier to follow. Keep it informal. It must not be a plan, a process, or a condition.

Red-team before finalizing:
(a) Read the revision as the colleague would on a bad day. Does any word still sound like contempt or public exposure?
(b) Read it as a skeptic who checks for "ruinous empathy". Would a listener know, without guessing, that the presentation was hard to follow? If the message got softened away, revise again.

Requirements (unchanged):
- Give exactly one revised sentence and a short explanation.
- Preserve the message that the presentation was difficult to follow.
- Do not invent what happened in the meeting.
- Do not diagnose the colleague: no claims about their nerves, preparation, ability, or intent.
- Do not suggest a performance-management process.

With my approval only: send the original and revised sentences, with no names or context, to a qualified independent reviewer (e.g., an HR partner or executive coach) to score them against the rubric below.

Judge, a rubric checked line by line against the final text before returning:
1. Exactly one sentence.
2. It states plainly that the presentation was hard to follow.
3. It contains no facts about the meeting beyond what the original sentence says.
4. It makes no claim about the colleague's state, traits, or motives.
5. It mentions no performance process.
6. It contains no "mess" verdict and no unverified claim about what everyone experienced.
7. Both red-team readings pass.

Pass condition: all 7 items hold. Items 1–6 can be checked by reading. Item 7 and the overall "candid without humiliating" quality are the author's judgment. Label them unverified unless the approved independent reviewer confirms them.
