Critique this sentence for a manager who wants to be candid without humiliating a colleague: "Your presentation was a mess and everyone was confused."

Goal: the sentence should do more than avoid humiliation. It should open a conversation that helps the colleague make the next presentation easier to follow. Assumption: the manager wants a follow-up conversation, not only a verdict.

Method:
- Separate observation from evaluation (Nonviolent Communication). "A mess" is a judgment of the work. "Difficult to follow" is the observable effect. Keep the effect and drop the label.
- Test the belief the sentence rests on: "everyone was confused." The manager can vouch for their own experience. A claim about the whole room is an overgeneralization the colleague can dispute, and it adds public shame. Phrase the claim as the manager's own experience, and do not add any detail about the audience that the original does not contain.
- Use the Situation–Behavior–Impact structure, and Radical Candor's test of "care personally, challenge directly." The difficulty must stay plainly stated: no compliment sandwich and no hedging that hides the point.
- Use politeness theory (Brown & Levinson). The criticism is a face-threatening act. Reduce the threat to the colleague's self-image by aiming the criticism at the presentation, not the person, and by inviting their view or offering joint work. Do not weaken the message itself.
- Red-team the result from two sides. First, read it as the colleague: does any word belittle them or suggest the whole room judged them? Second, read it as a skeptic of soft feedback: would the colleague still clearly understand that the presentation was hard to follow?

Requirements (unchanged):
- Give exactly one revised sentence and a short explanation.
- Preserve the message that the presentation was difficult to follow.
- Do not invent what happened in the meeting.
- Do not diagnose the colleague, including their motives, skills, nerves, or preparation.
- Do not suggest a performance-management process.
- Apply the checks below before answering, and do not add sections beyond the sentence and explanation.

(1) the output is exactly one sentence;
(2) the sentence explicitly says the presentation was hard to follow;

(4) it contains no words about the colleague's traits, abilities, or state of mind;
(5) it mentions no formal process;
(6) it invites a next step, such as the colleague's view or working together;
(7) the explanation is short and names the specific changes from the original.
These checks are the author's own, so the claim that the sentence is "not humiliating" stays unverified until the owner's reviewer reads it.
With my approval only: the owner shows the original and the revision, unlabeled, to a trusted reader who was not in the meeting. The reader judges which version more clearly says the presentation was hard to follow and which version is less humiliating. The revision passes if it wins or ties on clarity and wins on dignity.
