```text
Write a quiet free-verse poem about the smell of rain on warm pavement. It is for its own sake, not for marketing or publication. Use plain language. Keep it under 120 words. No rhyme scheme. No explanatory note after the poem: deliver only the poem.

Work the way a careful poet would. Start from the raw sensory moment, not the idea of "rain smell": the heat held in the pavement before the first drops, the first scattered dark spots, the smell rising before the ground is fully wet, the change once it is soaked. Choose two or three exact physical particulars, such as a specific surface, time of day, or sound, over general statements. Let the smell arrive through what it does, not through adjectives like "fresh" or "earthy".

Borrow a perfumer's method: smell unfolds in stages, a sharp first note, a fuller middle, a lingering base. Let the poem follow the smell through time, from the first drops to the soaked street, so the poem moves rather than describes one frozen moment. Keep any science out of the language. No words like "petrichor" or "geosmin". The poem should stay plain.

Draft, then revise by cutting. Remove any line that explains a feeling instead of showing a thing. Remove words that only add sound. Read it aloud. Wherever the quiet breaks, with a raised voice, an exclamation, or a big closing statement, soften or cut that line. End on an image, not a moral.

Judge: count the words with `wc -w` or an equivalent. The count must be under 120. List the final word of each line and confirm there is no repeating end-rhyme pattern. Confirm the reply contains the poem and nothing after it. The owner's own reading decides whether the poem is quiet and true to the smell. Until they read it, that quality is unverified.
```
