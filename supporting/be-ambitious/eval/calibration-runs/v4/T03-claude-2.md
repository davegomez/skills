```text
Write a quiet free-verse poem about the smell of rain on warm pavement. It is for its own sake, not for marketing or publication. Use plain language. Keep it under 120 words. Use no rhyme scheme. Reply with the poem only: no explanatory note, process notes, or check results after it.

Assumed aim: a reader who knows this smell should recognize it from the poem alone, rather than reading a poem about rain in general.

Start from the raw moment, not the stock image. Before drafting, privately list 10 to 15 concrete particulars of the moment. Include what the first drops look like on dry concrete, the heat still rising, the dust-and-tar smell, the wet-stone smell after it, the sounds, the light, the time of day, and what a person is doing when they notice it. Stay true to how the event works: the smell is strongest when rain hits ground that has been dry and hot, and it rises from the ground rather than falling from the sky. Keep the science out of the words, so no "petrichor" and no "geosmin."

Borrow the perfumer's method of describing a scent in stages: the opening, the middle, and what lingers. Let the poem follow the smell over a minute or two. Anchor each stage to a concrete thing it resembles instead of adjectives like "fresh" or "earthy."

Draft three short versions with different openings. Keep the one with the most specific, true detail, then revise it. Cut every word the poem can do without. Replace abstractions with things. Keep the tone low: no storm drama, no exclamation, no closing moral. Let the line breaks set the pace. Go through it line by line for sound, and fix any line that stumbles or sounds fancy.

First check: confirm that a shell is available for counting words. If it is not, count by hand twice.

With my approval only: give the poem alone, with no title or context, to a fresh subagent as a cold reader. Ask what smell and what surface it describes, and which line sounds loudest.

Judge, before replying:
- Word count: pipe the final text to `wc -w`. Pass: under 120.
- Rhyme: list the last word of each line. Pass: no regular end-rhyme pattern.
- Plain language: Pass: no word an ordinary reader would need to look up.
- Format: Pass: nothing follows the poem.
- Cold read, if approved: Pass if the reader names rain on warm pavement, concrete, or asphalt without prompting. Revise the line they flag as loudest.
Whether the poem moves its reader stays unverified until the owner reads it.
```
