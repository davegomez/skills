```text
Critique this sentence for a manager who wants to be candid without humiliating a colleague: "Your presentation was a mess and everyone was confused."
Give one revised sentence and a short explanation. Preserve the message that the presentation was difficult to follow. Do not invent what happened in the meeting, diagnose the colleague, or suggest a performance-management process.

Goal: write a sentence the colleague can hear without losing face, can't easily dismiss, and can act on.
Assumption: the manager wants the colleague's next presentation to be easier to follow, so the sentence should open a short conversation about that. It should not close the topic with a verdict.

Method:
- Separate observation from evaluation (nonviolent communication). "Mess" is a verdict on the work as a whole. "Difficult to follow" is an observable effect. Keep the effect and drop the verdict.
- Shape the sentence as Situation–Behavior–Impact. Use only the situation and impact the original supports: the presentation, and that it was hard to follow. Add no behaviors, slides, or reactions.
- Check what the speaker can actually claim. "Everyone was confused" speaks for people who did not say so, and the colleague can easily dispute it. Have the manager speak from their own position ("I found it hard to follow") or soften the scope ("it was hard to follow"). Do not assert facts about the room.
- Apply politeness theory (Brown & Levinson). Treat the feedback as a face-threatening act. Aim the criticism at the presentation, not the person, and use no intensifiers or global words like "mess" or "everyone." Hedges must not blur the message.
- Optionally end with a forward-looking invitation, such as asking to talk about making the next one easier to follow. It must not imply a formal process.
- Red-team the draft. Read it as the colleague would on a bad day and name the most hurtful plausible reading. If one exists, revise. Also read it as a skeptic and check whether the "difficult to follow" message survives. If it is hedged away, revise.

Deliverable: exactly one revised sentence, then a short explanation (2–4 sentences) of what changed and why.

With my approval only: show the revised sentence, without context, to one experienced manager or HR partner and ask whether it reads as both candid and respectful.

Judge: check each item yes/no and fix any "no" before returning.
(1) Exactly one sentence.
(2) It still clearly says the presentation was difficult to follow.
(3) No invented meeting details.
(4) No diagnosis of the colleague's skills, effort, or state.
(5) No performance-management process.
(6) No global evaluative words aimed at the person or the whole work.
(7) The red-team reading finds no humiliating interpretation.
Passes when all 7 are yes. Without the independent reviewer, the "respectful" quality is author-checked only and unverified.
```

- `altitude`: the original outcome was a gentler sentence. The improved outcome is a sentence that opens a conversation about the next presentation. It stays within one sentence and suggests no process.
- `depth`: separating observation from evaluation, using SBI shape, and checking what the speaker can claim. The last one tests the "everyone was confused" belief, which the manager can't verify and the colleague can easily rebut.
- `range`: politeness theory (face-threatening acts) and a two-sided red-team: the hurtful reading, and the risk of over-hedging until the message disappears.
- `range`/judge: a 7-item checklist covers every explicit constraint. An optional independent reviewer can assess tone, but needs approval because it shares the sentence with someone else.
