```text
Critique this sentence for a manager who wants to be candid without humiliating a colleague: "Your presentation was a mess and everyone was confused."

Deliverable: one revised sentence and a short explanation. Nothing else.

Requirements:
- Preserve the message that the presentation was difficult to follow. If the revision is softened until that message disappears, it fails.
- Do not invent what happened in the meeting. No specific slides, sections, moments, or reactions that the original sentence does not state.
- Do not diagnose the colleague. No guesses about preparation, nerves, skill, or motives.
- Do not suggest a performance-management process.

Assumption: the manager wants the colleague to be able to respond or act, not only to receive a verdict. Aim the revised sentence at that. It should state the difficulty plainly and leave an opening, such as a question or an offer, that the colleague can take up. It should not name a plan, a follow-up meeting, or anything that reads as a process.

Method:
1. Diagnose the original word by word before rewriting. For each part, name what it does:
   - "Your presentation": makes the person the owner of the failure. Check whether the revision can address the presentation without passing a verdict on the person.
   - "a mess": a global evaluation, not an observation. Nonviolent Communication separates observation from evaluation. "Difficult to follow" is closer to an observation of the listener's experience.
   - "everyone was confused": an overgeneralization. It claims to know every attendee's mind and turns the criticism public. The original sentence is the only evidence, so do not assert more about others' confusion than it does. Prefer the manager's own experience ("I found it hard to follow"), which the manager can stand behind. If you keep a reference to others, attribute it without "everyone" and without implying evidence the original does not give.
2. Use the Situation–Behavior–Impact (SBI) feedback model to check structure: the situation (the presentation), the observation (it was difficult to follow), and the impact on the listener. Fill only the slots the original supports, and leave the rest out rather than invent them.
3. Use politeness theory (Brown and Levinson's face-threatening acts) to locate the humiliation. Identify which words threaten the colleague's positive face (being judged incompetent, in front of others) and which threaten negative face (being ordered to change). Remove the threat from the framing, not from the message. Do not hedge the core claim with "a bit", "maybe", or "some people might have" if that weakens "difficult to follow".
4. Draft at least three candidate sentences that vary in how directly they state the difficulty and how well they protect face. Choose the one that best holds both.
5. Red-team the chosen sentence twice before finalizing:
   - Read it as the colleague would the morning after. Does any word read as contempt, public shaming, or a judgment of them as a person?
   - Read it as a skeptic of softening. Could the colleague conclude from this that the presentation was fine? If yes, the candor was lost, so revise.
6. Write the short explanation from the diagnosis: what in the original would humiliate, what the revision changes, and why the core message survives. Keep it short. Do not append the candidate drafts, scores, or rubric.

With my approval only: share the revised sentence with a trusted peer who reads it cold as an independent reviewer.

Judge: before returning, check the revised sentence against this pass/fail rubric:
  (a) It is one sentence.
  (b) It states plainly that the presentation was difficult to follow, so a reader cannot conclude it went fine.
  (c) It contains no global evaluative label of the work or the person ("mess", "bad", "terrible").
  (d) It claims no attendee reactions, meeting events, or details beyond the original sentence.
  (e) It contains no diagnosis of the colleague and no performance-management step.
  (f) It leaves the colleague an opening to respond or act.
This is the author's own rubric. It guides revision but does not verify the result.
Pass condition: all six items pass in the author's check. Then the manager, or an approved reviewer, reads the sentence as the recipient would and confirms (b), (c), and (f). Until that reading happens, the sentence's effect on the colleague is unverified.
```
