```text
Critique this sentence for a manager who wants to be candid without humiliating a colleague: "Your presentation was a mess and everyone was confused."

Deliverable: one revised sentence and a short explanation. Nothing more.

Hard requirements:
- Preserve the message that the presentation was difficult to follow. Do not soften it into a hint, a compliment sandwich, or a hedge the colleague could miss.
- Do not invent what happened in the meeting. Use no details, examples, or audience reactions that the original sentence does not contain.
- Do not diagnose the colleague, including their motives, skills, nerves, or preparation.
- Do not suggest a performance-management process.

Assumption: the sentence's real job is to open a conversation that helps the colleague make the next presentation easier to follow, not only to deliver a verdict. Where the one-sentence limit allows, the revision should leave a clear, non-procedural door open to that next step, such as an offer to talk it through. It must not be a formal plan or process.

Method:
1. Separate observation from evaluation, as in Nonviolent Communication. Find each word that judges the work or the person rather than describing an effect. "A mess" is a global verdict. Replace it with the observable effect: the presentation was hard to follow.
2. Apply the Situation-Behavior-Impact feedback model only as far as the original supports. Keep the impact, which is that the talk was difficult to follow. Add no situation or behavior details, because none are given.
3. Check the scope of each claim. "Everyone was confused" speaks for an audience and turns private feedback into public exposure. Restate the claim as something the manager can stand behind, such as the manager's own experience of following it. Keep the message itself at full strength. In the explanation, note in one clause that the manager can add specifics only if they actually observed them.
4. Use politeness theory (Brown & Levinson's face-threatening acts). Keep the force of the criticism while removing what threatens the colleague's standing: "everyone," the totalizing noun, and the framing of the work as a reflection of the person.
5. Red-team the draft from two sides before finalizing:
   (a) Read it as the colleague would. What is the most humiliating reading it still allows?
   (b) Read it as a skeptic. Could a defensive reader conclude the presentation was fine?
   Revise until both readings fail.

Explanation: keep it short. Name what the original does wrong (verdict instead of effect, overclaimed "everyone", audience-as-witness), what the revision changes, and why it stays candid.

First check: whether an independent reviewer is available to judge the result. If none is, report the revision as unverified against the judge below. The rubric in step 5 guides revision but does not count as verification.

With my approval only: share the original and revised sentences with an independent reviewer (a trusted peer or HR partner, not the colleague) so they can apply the judge below.

Judge: an independent reviewer reads the revised sentence without seeing the explanation.
Pass when all of these hold:
(1) The reviewer paraphrases its message as "the presentation was hard to follow" or equivalent.
(2) The reviewer does not find it humiliating, publicly shaming, or a personal verdict.
(3) The reviewer does not find it so soft that the message could be missed.
(4) The reviewer finds no invented meeting details, no diagnosis of the colleague, and no performance-management suggestion.
Until a reviewer runs this check, the revision is unverified.
```
