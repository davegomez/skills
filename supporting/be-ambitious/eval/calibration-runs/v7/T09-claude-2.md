```text
Critique this sentence for a manager who wants to be candid without humiliating a colleague: "Your presentation was a mess and everyone was confused."

Assumption: the manager will say or send this to the colleague, and the goal is a sentence the colleague can hear without losing face, can act on, and that leaves room for a next conversation.

Method, applied only to the sentence's own words:
- Separate observation from evaluation (the distinction used in Nonviolent Communication). "Was a mess" is a verdict on the work. "Everyone was confused" claims to know what other people experienced. Keep what the manager can stand behind, which is their own experience of the presentation, and state it directly.
- Find the face threats, using politeness theory (Brown & Levinson). "Mess" attacks the colleague's competence. "Everyone" turns a private remark into a report of public failure. Remove the threat without removing the message.
- Separate the work from the person. Check whether "your" makes the sentence about the colleague instead of the presentation.
- Keep it candid. Test the revision against the opposite failure: if a hedge, a compliment sandwich, or vagueness would let the colleague miss that the presentation was hard to follow, it fails.
- If it still fits in one natural sentence, end with an opening toward improvement, such as an offer to talk it through. This must not be a plan, a warning, or anything that reads as a formal process.
- Red-team the draft as the colleague would read it on a bad day. Flag any sarcasm, any condescension, any implied audience of witnesses, and any ambiguity about whether the presentation was hard to follow. Revise until none remain.

Requirements:
- Give one revised sentence and a short explanation. Nothing else.
- Preserve the message that the presentation was difficult to follow.
- Do not invent what happened in the meeting. No specific slides, moments, or reactions.
- Do not diagnose the colleague. No guesses about causes, preparation, nerves, or skill.
- Do not suggest a performance-management process.

Judge: a reviewer other than the author, chosen by the manager and shown only the original sentence, the revision, and the explanation, checks these criteria:
(1) The revision clearly says the presentation was hard to follow.
(2) It contains no insult, no claim about what others thought, and no invented detail.
(3) It contains no diagnosis and no performance-management language.
(4) The reviewer, reading as the colleague, would feel addressed respectfully rather than exposed.
(5) The output is exactly one sentence plus a short explanation.
Pass only if every criterion is met.
```

- `altitude`: The original outcome was a less harsh sentence. The improved outcome is a sentence the colleague can act on, with an optional opening to a follow-up conversation. It stays one sentence and avoids any process language.
- `depth`: The prompt replaces a general "make it nicer" with named methods (the observation/evaluation split and work/person separation) applied to the raw wording. It also adds an explicit check that softening hasn't erased the message.
- `range`: Politeness theory adds face-threat analysis, which shows how "mess" and "everyone" do the damage. The bad-day reading by the colleague adds a red-team check that catches sarcasm or ambiguity that a quick edit misses.
- `judge`: The prompt adds an independent reviewer with a five-point rubric. Cost: the manager shares two unattributed sentences with one person they trust, with no names or meeting details.
- Skipped: none. Each lens adds a distinct permitted contribution.
- Owner actions: choose a reviewer for the judge, or accept the result as unverified. Decide whether the optional follow-up opening fits your relationship with the colleague.
