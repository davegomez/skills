Prepare the quarterly business review deck for our leadership meeting in two weeks. Leaders must use it to decide whether to invest in retention or acquisition. The deck is required and must contain at most 12 slides, including any appendix.

Use existing staff and tools. Do not change our operating cadence or contact customers. Recommend a decision; do not execute investments or introduce ongoing workflows. Deliver only the deck, with supporting evidence and qualifications contained in its slides or speaker notes.

Assumption: leaders need a defensible investment choice, not merely a retrospective performance summary.
Assumption: retention and acquisition should be compared on incremental economic value over a common time horizon, subject to leadership’s actual budget, cash, and risk constraints. Confirm these criteria rather than silently treating them as settled.

First check dependencies:
- The raw customer and revenue exports have been offered but not shared. Confirm their availability, coverage, definitions, and permitted use before analyzing them.
- Check whether existing, authorized sources also contain acquisition spending, retention costs, gross margins, and relevant historical interventions. Do not assume those inputs exist or seek new access.
- Verify that existing tools can produce the deck and perform the analysis. Confirm which already-authorized leadership reviewer, other than the author, can judge its decision usefulness.
- Identify missing inputs. Continue only with supported analysis, labeling assumptions, estimates, and unverified conclusions. If essential data remain unavailable, still produce the required deck, explicitly showing which decision cannot yet be supported.

Build the deck around a decision, not a sequence of departmental updates:
1. State the retention-versus-acquisition question, the recommended choice if supported, and the conditions under which that recommendation changes.
2. Establish the quarterly baseline from the raw exports. Reconcile customer and revenue totals, distinguish new, retained, expanded, contracted, and lost revenue where the data support those categories, and disclose definition or coverage gaps.
3. Use customer-cohort analysis to separate lifecycle effects from changes in customer mix. Show customer retention and revenue retention separately where possible; do not treat revenue growth alone as evidence of healthy retention.
4. Test the premise that improving the weaker historical metric is necessarily the better investment. Historical churn or acquisition performance does not establish the incremental return from spending more.
5. Use marginal unit economics to compare feasible retention and acquisition scenarios at comparable spending levels and over the same horizon. Show incremental gross profit, investment cost, and cash/payback timing where supported. Separate observed performance from assumed intervention effects; do not present correlation as causal lift.
6. Use decision analysis: calculate the break-even improvement each option needs, then test how plausible ranges for costs, conversion, churn reduction, and customer value change the preferred option. Use only variables supported by the available data or explicitly labeled assumptions.
7. Red-team the recommendation by constructing the strongest evidence-supported case for the opposite choice. Identify the assumption most likely to reverse the decision and show its switching threshold.
8. End with the precise decision leaders are being asked to make, the supporting evidence, and any unresolved uncertainty material to that decision. Do not manufacture certainty to force a recommendation.

Keep exhibits traceable to source periods, definitions, and calculations. Put detailed qualifications and reproducible calculation descriptions in speaker notes rather than adding a companion report. Report measured findings as measured, scenarios as scenarios, and missing evidence as unverified.

Judge: existing-tool checks must confirm that the deck opens, contains no more than 12 slides including any appendix, and that displayed totals and scenario calculations reconcile to their stated inputs. An already-authorized leadership decision-maker other than the author checks whether the deck clearly states the decision, compares the options on consistent terms, distinguishes evidence from assumptions, exposes the strongest countercase, and identifies conditions that would reverse the recommendation. Pass only if every check succeeds; absent data or reviewer access leaves the affected conclusion or review unverified, not passed.
