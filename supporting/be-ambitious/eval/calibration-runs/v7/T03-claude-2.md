```text
Write a quiet free-verse poem about the smell of rain on warm pavement. It is for its own sake, not for marketing or publication.

Assumption: the aim is a poem that brings the smell back to a reader who has stood in that moment. The reader should almost smell it, not be told about it.

Before drafting, gather raw material the way a poet keeps a notebook. List 15–20 specific physical details of the moment from ordinary experience: the first drops darkening the pavement in coin-sized spots, heat still rising, dust, tar, wet stone, a nearby lawn or gutter, the sound, what the air does afterward. Keep the plainest and least expected details. Drop any you have met in a hundred rain poems.

Follow the smell through time, the way a perfumer describes a scent by its opening, heart, and drydown. First comes the sharp breath when drops hit hot ground. Then the fuller smell as the pavement darkens. Last comes the cooler, fainter smell as the rain settles in. Let this change give the poem its shape instead of a stated idea. If you know the science of the smell, use it only to get the sequence right, never as vocabulary.

Write at least two full drafts, then revise like an editor:
- Cut adjectives and abstractions that a concrete image can replace.
- Prefer nouns and verbs.
- End on an image, not a statement of feeling.
- Read each line aloud in your head. Break lines where a speaker would pause or breathe.
- Keep the tone quiet: no exclamations, no grand claims.

Requirements:
- Plain language.
- Under 120 words.
- Free verse with no rhyme scheme.
- No explanatory note after the poem.

Before replying, check silently and do not report the checks:
- Count the words, using `wc -w` if a shell is available, otherwise counting twice by hand. Confirm the total is under 120.
- Read the line-end words and confirm they form no rhyme pattern.

Judge: the owner, reading the poem aloud, checks five things:
1. The tone stays quiet.
2. The language is plain, with nothing they would need to look up.
3. Concrete details let them smell the scene; the poem does not explain it.
4. The smell changes across the poem.
5. It ends on an image.
Pass only if every criterion is met, the poem is under 120 words, it has no rhyme scheme, and nothing follows it. The poem's quality stays unverified until the owner reads it.
```

- `depth`: Starting from a notebook of raw sensory details before drafting replaces stock rain imagery with specific, unexpected detail. No scope or cost change.
- `depth`: Two drafts, an editor's cuts, line breaks tested by reading aloud, and an image ending give quiet craft discipline. The word-count and rhyme self-check enforces your hard limits without adding any note to the output.
- `range`: The perfumer's opening, heart, and drydown structure gives the poem a shape in time, so it follows how the smell actually changes rather than listing images. Science informs the order of events only, which keeps the language plain.
- Skipped: `altitude`, because the poem is the end in itself and drives no later decision or action. Hard-target move, because the only measurable bar is your 120-word limit, which stays fixed. Red-team, because there is no consequential claim to test.
- Owner actions: read the poem aloud against the five judge criteria. No approvals or access are needed.
