```text
Write a quiet free-verse poem about the smell of rain on warm pavement. It is for its own sake, not for marketing or publication.

Assumption: the goal is a poem that brings back the actual smell and the small moment around it, so a reader feels it rather than being told about it.

Start from the raw sensation, not the usual summary of it. Before drafting, privately list concrete particulars of that moment: what the air smells like in the minute before rain, at the first scattered drops on hot concrete, and after the pavement has gone dark and cooled; the heat coming up from the ground; sounds, light, and what a person might be doing there. Some of that smell comes from oils and dust the dry ground has held, lifted by the first drops. Use this as material only. Keep scientific terms out of the poem.

Borrow from perfumery: a scent unfolds over time, from a sharp first impression to a middle to what lingers. Let the poem follow how the smell changes over those few minutes, so its shape comes from the smell itself.

Draft, then revise the way working poets do: read each line aloud, cut abstractions and stock phrases about rain, keep only words a person would say plainly, and let line breaks set a slow, quiet pace. End on an image, not a conclusion.

Requirements:
- Free verse, no rhyme scheme.
- Plain language.
- Under 120 words.
- Output the poem only. No title note, no explanation, and no commentary before or after it.

Before returning, count the words with a tool, or count them by hand if no tool is available, and confirm the total is under 120. Check that there is no end-rhyme pattern. Do not include either check in the output.

Judge: the owner reads the poem aloud. Pass only if all of these hold: it is under 120 words; it has no rhyme scheme; it uses plain language; it sounds quiet; it evokes the smell of rain on warm pavement through concrete detail; and nothing follows the poem.
```
