```text
Prepare the quarterly business review deck for our leadership meeting in two weeks. Leaders must use it to decide whether to invest in retention or acquisition. The deck is required and must be at most 12 slides. Use existing staff and tools. Do not change our operating cadence or contact customers. Deliver only the deck; put material findings, assumptions, limitations, and validation results within its 12-slide limit.

Assumption: the deck should support a defensible allocation decision, not merely summarize quarterly performance.
Assumption: compare retention and acquisition on a common investment basis and time horizon, subject to leadership’s actual constraints; do not assume a budget or implementation authority.

First check dependencies:
- Request the raw customer and revenue exports, which have not yet been shared. Confirm their coverage, field definitions, reporting period, and permitted use.
- Establish the decision horizon, feasible resource envelope, and which relevant cost or margin information is already available through authorized sources. Do not assume new access.
- Check that an existing tool can reproduce the calculations and that the owner, acting as a leadership decision-maker independent of the deck’s author, can review decision readiness.
- If dependencies remain missing, identify the affected conclusions as unverified. Build only what available evidence supports; never substitute invented business results.

Make the deck a decision instrument:
- Lead with the decision, the evidence-supported recommendation, and the conditions that would reverse it. If evidence cannot distinguish the options, say so and identify the decision-critical unknown.
- Compare retention and acquisition using the same horizon, explicit assumptions, and comparable incremental investment. Separate observed results from scenario estimates.
- Show expected incremental contribution after relevant costs where supported. Revenue alone must not stand in for profitability; unavailable costs must remain visible limitations.
- Identify the smallest existing-data check that would most reduce a decision-critical uncertainty. Do not initiate a new workflow or experiment.

Work from raw evidence:
- Use reproducible queries, spreadsheet formulas, or scripts in existing tools. Check customer identifiers, duplicates, missingness, date coverage, refunds, and revenue definitions; reconcile totals to supplied sources and explain material discrepancies.
- Use acquisition-cohort analysis to distinguish customer mix and customer age from retention performance. Compare equivalent observation windows so newer cohorts are not treated as fully observed.
- Separate retained-customer revenue, expansion or contraction, and new-customer revenue where the exports support those distinctions.
- Test the premise that the weaker headline metric identifies the better investment: distinguish the size of a problem from the marginal return achievable by spending on it.
- Use observed unit economics where available. If acquisition costs, retention intervention costs, margins, or response estimates are absent, show explicitly labeled break-even scenarios rather than presenting estimated returns as measured facts.

Borrow methods that expose decision risk:
- Decision analysis: calculate the break-even assumptions at which the preferred option changes, and test plausible ranges to show whether the recommendation is robust or fragile.
- Causal inference: distinguish historical associations from intervention effects; flag selection bias, cohort-mix effects, and unsupported causal claims.
- Adversarial review: make the strongest evidence-based case for the opposite allocation, then check whether the recommendation survives. Use this to expose consequential downside and omitted constraints, not to manufacture certainty.

Prioritize executive readability: a clear decision headline, legible evidence, concise source references, and visible uncertainty. Fit the analysis to the deck rather than adding a companion report.

Judge: use existing tools to verify the slide count and reproduce material calculations from the supplied sources. The owner independently reviews the deck as a leadership decision-maker. Pass requires at most 12 slides, reconciled or explicitly qualified figures, clear separation of observations and assumptions, and a comparison on a common basis. The owner must be able to identify the recommended choice—or the specific reason a choice is not yet defensible—the supporting economics, principal downside, and reversal threshold from the deck alone. Until these checks run, leave the affected calculations and decision-readiness claims unverified.
```
