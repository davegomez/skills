```text
Write a quiet free-verse poem about the smell of rain on warm pavement. It is for its own sake, not for marketing or publication.

Assumption: "quiet" means low volume in sound and claim. Small, exact noticing, with no big statements about memory, renewal, or nature.

Method:
- Start from the raw sensory moment, not the idea of it. Before drafting, privately list concrete particulars of the first minutes of rain on sun-heated pavement: the order the smell arrives in, what it is made of (dust, hot stone, tar, wet grass at the edges), what the body does (breathing in, slowing down), and what the eye and ear catch (dark coin-sized spots, steam, the sound changing).
- Borrow the perfumer's way of noticing. Separate the smell into what arrives first, what stays, and what lingers after. Name each part by its plain source ("hot dust," "wet stone"), not by adjectives or technical terms. Keep the words "petrichor," "notes," and any other jargon out of the poem.
- Pick one moment and one place, and let the poem stay there.
- Draft, then revise by cutting. Remove abstractions, explanations, and any line that tells the reader what to feel. Read it aloud and adjust the line breaks so the pauses fall where a breath would.

Requirements:
- Free verse with no rhyme scheme.
- Plain language.
- Under 120 words.
- Output the poem only. A title is optional. Put no explanatory note, commentary, or check results before or after it.

Judge, run silently before output:
1. The word count of the poem (title included) is under 120.
2. The line-ending words show no recurring end-rhyme pattern.
3. Nothing follows the last line.
4. Self-rubric, used only to guide revision: every image is concrete and sensory, there is no jargon or abstraction, and the tone stays quiet.

Pass condition: checks 1 to 3 hold. The poem's quality stays unverified until the owner reads it.
```
