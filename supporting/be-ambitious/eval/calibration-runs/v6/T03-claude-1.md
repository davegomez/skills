```text
Write a quiet free-verse poem about the smell of rain on warm pavement, for its own sake. It is not for marketing or publication.

Assumption: the reader is you, the owner, so the poem should be true to the experience, not impressive.

Method:
1. Before drafting, list 8–12 raw sensory particulars of the moment. What is on the pavement: dust, tar, oil, chalk, leaves? Note the heat before the rain, the first dark spots, the sound, the light, what the body does. Draft from this list. Do not start from the idea of "petrichor". Use concrete nouns and verbs, and keep the scientific or fancy word for the smell out of the poem.
2. Borrow a perfumer's way of describing a scent over time: the first sharp note when the drops hit, the fuller middle as the pavement darkens, and the faint trace left as the ground cools. Let the poem move through these stages, or through one of them closely. Do not use perfume terms in the poem.
3. Revise by cutting. Remove any adjective that a noun or verb already implies. Remove lines that explain what the reader already feels. End on an image, not a statement. Read it aloud and smooth any line that sounds louder than the rest.
4. Check the end words of all lines for an accidental rhyme pattern, and break any you find.

Requirements: plain language; free verse with no rhyme scheme; under 120 words; quiet in tone. Output only the poem, with no title note, explanation, or commentary after it.

First check: if a word-counting tool is available, use it on the final text. If not, count by hand, line by line.

Judge: before replying, the final text is under 120 words, has no end-rhyme pattern, and nothing follows the last line of the poem. Author's revision rubric, a guide and not proof: every line holds a concrete particular or a turn, and no line explains the feeling. Whether the poem moves you can only be judged when you read it, so it stays unverified until then.
```
