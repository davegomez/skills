```text
Critique this sentence for a manager who wants to be candid without humiliating a colleague: "Your presentation was a mess and everyone was confused."

Goal: one sentence the manager can say privately that the colleague can hear without losing face and can act on. It should be clear enough that the colleague knows the presentation was hard to follow, and open enough that they want to talk about how to fix it.
Assumption: the manager will say this one-on-one, not in front of others.
Assumption: the manager wants the colleague's next presentation to be easier to follow, not just to register disapproval.

Method:
- Separate the work from the person. "A mess" judges the colleague's competence globally. Name the observable effect instead: the presentation was hard to follow.
- Speak only for what the manager can vouch for. "Everyone was confused" claims knowledge of every attendee's mind and implies a public verdict. Use the manager's own experience ("I found it hard to follow") or a claim the manager can stand behind. Do not add details about the meeting that the task does not supply.
- Apply politeness theory (Brown & Levinson). Criticism threatens the colleague's "face", their public self-image. Lower the threat by removing the insult and the audience, not by hedging the message into vagueness. Candor survives; humiliation does not.
- Pair the observation with an invitation that points forward, such as an offer to go through it together. This must not be a plan, a process, or a judgment of the person.
- Red-team the draft twice. First, read it as the colleague would: what would sting, and what would feel evasive? Second, read it as if a third party overheard it: does it still sound fair? Revise until both readings hold.

Requirements:
- Output exactly one revised sentence and a short explanation of why it works better than the original. The explanation carries the critique.
- Preserve the message that the presentation was difficult to follow.
- Do not invent what happened in the meeting.
- Do not diagnose the colleague: no motives, traits, nerves, preparation, or skill gaps.
- Do not suggest a performance-management process.

Before returning, check the draft against the rubric below and revise any item that fails. This self-check guides revision only; it is not the verdict.

Judge: an independent reviewer (someone experienced in giving workplace feedback, chosen by the manager, who did not write the revision) scores the sentence and explanation against this rubric:
(1) The difficult-to-follow message is unmistakable.
(2) There is no insult or global judgment of the person.
(3) It makes no claims about others' reactions or meeting events beyond what was supplied.
(4) It contains no diagnosis of the colleague.
(5) It contains no performance-management process.
(6) The output is exactly one sentence plus a short explanation.
(7) The reviewer would be willing to say it to a colleague themselves.
Pass: all 7 items yes. Until the reviewer scores it, the revision is unverified.
```
