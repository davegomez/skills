```text
Critique this sentence for a manager who wants to be candid without humiliating a colleague: "Your presentation was a mess and everyone was confused."
Give one revised sentence and a short explanation. Preserve the message that the presentation was difficult to follow. Do not invent what happened in the meeting, diagnose the colleague, or suggest a performance-management process.

Goal: a sentence the colleague can hear without losing face and can act on in their next presentation, while the manager still says plainly that it was hard to follow.
Assumption: the manager will say or write this sentence directly to the colleague, and wants them to improve future presentations, not just to be told.

Method:
- Separate the work from the person. "A mess" judges the whole effort. Name the property of the presentation that failed, which is that it was hard to follow, and leave the colleague's competence out of it.
- Test the claim the sentence rests on. "Everyone was confused" asserts what other people experienced, and the task gives no evidence for it. Speak only from what the manager can own, such as "I found it hard to follow," unless the task supplies evidence about others. Do not add specifics about slides, sections, or reactions, because none are given.
- Apply politeness theory (Brown and Levinson). Criticism threatens the listener's face, and a public generalization like "everyone" threatens it more. Reduce the threat with first-person framing and scope limited to this one presentation. Keep the message itself intact: no hedging so heavy that "hard to follow" disappears.
- Point forward. Where it fits in one sentence, end with an opening that helps the colleague act on the feedback, such as an offer to talk it through. This must not be a plan, a process, or a condition.
- Red-team the draft twice before finalizing:
  - Read it as the colleague would. Is anything still humiliating, sarcastic, or condescending?
  - Read it as a skeptic would. Has the candor been softened so much that the message is lost, or has it drifted into invention, diagnosis, or process?
  Revise until both readings pass.

Requirements (unchanged): exactly one revised sentence plus a short explanation; preserve "difficult to follow"; no invented meeting details, no diagnosis of the colleague, no performance-management suggestion.

Judge: an independent reviewer experienced in giving workplace feedback scores the sentence against this rubric:
(1) states clearly that the presentation was hard to follow;
(2) addresses the work, not the person;
(3) claims only what the manager could know firsthand;
(4) contains no invented details, diagnosis, or process;
(5) a recipient would find it candid but not humiliating;
(6) it is one sentence.
Pass: all six met. Until a reviewer applies the rubric, treat the result as the author's draft, not verified.
```
