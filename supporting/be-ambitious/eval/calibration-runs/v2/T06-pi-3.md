### Rewritten prompt

```text
Help me understand why customers churned last month so I can decide what to investigate or change—not merely repeat the sales team’s explanation.

Known inputs:
- I have no raw event logs, CRM export, or database access to share right now.
- The sales team thinks onboarding is the cause. This is a reported belief, not verified evidence of causation.
- Do not buy data, scrape customer information, or request production credentials.

Assumption: I want to prioritize retention work. Confirm this before expanding into intervention design; do not implement changes or contact customers.

First establish what can actually be known:
1. Ask only the minimum clarifying questions: which calendar month and timezone “last month” means; whether churn means customer cancellation, non-renewal, or lost revenue; and whether any existing, approved, non-sensitive aggregate reports or anonymized cancellation feedback are available. Do not assume these exist or require unavailable records to proceed.
2. Separate supplied facts, reported opinions, assumptions, hypotheses, and missing evidence. Do not invent customer counts, reasons, quotations, percentages, or confidence estimates.
3. If no additional evidence is available, state plainly that the causes cannot yet be determined. Produce the bounded diagnostic brief below rather than an unsupported explanation.

Deliver a concise diagnostic brief for me:

A. What we know—and do not know
State the definition and period if confirmed; otherwise label them unresolved. Explain why “sales suspects onboarding” does not establish why last month’s customers churned. Distinguish the reasons customers left from the separate question of whether churn increased.

B. Competing explanations and discriminating checks
Treat onboarding as one hypothesis alongside a small set of credible alternatives, such as poor customer fit, failure to realize ongoing value, product reliability, pricing, and renewal timing. Label every alternative as proposed, not observed.

Use causal inference to turn each hypothesis into:
- an expected observable pattern;
- evidence that would weaken it;
- the smallest useful evidence request or check;
- important confounders or selection biases.

For onboarding, test whether the hypothesis predicts early-tenure churn or failure to reach first value. Explain how comparing churners with retained customers of similar tenure, segment, and acquisition period would be more informative than reviewing churners alone. Such a comparison tests association; do not call it causal proof.

C. Challenge the leading belief
Use a red-team counterexample test: identify patterns that would make onboarding an unlikely primary explanation—for example, churn concentrated among long-established customers who successfully activated. Use renewal-cohort accounting to distinguish a change in churn risk from simply having more contracts eligible to renew.

Where qualitative feedback becomes available, propose non-leading critical-incident questions about what happened before cancellation, rather than asking customers to confirm that onboarding was bad. Any new outreach, sensitive-data handling, or access requires my approval first.

D. The next decision
Rank a small number of evidence-gathering steps by their ability to distinguish competing explanations relative to effort, using qualitative judgments rather than invented scores. Specify what each possible result would change about the investigation. Do not recommend an onboarding overhaul solely on the sales team’s belief.

Push beyond confirmation: actively seek evidence that could overturn the onboarding explanation. If one evidence source is unavailable, identify a lower-access alternative and its limitations; do not bypass access restrictions. Keep the work to this diagnostic brief unless I approve broader analysis, outreach, spending, or implementation.

Judge and observable pass condition:
For this brief, provide a claim-to-evidence ledger: every factual attribution must point to supplied evidence, and every unsupported explanation must remain explicitly a hypothesis. With the current inputs, passing means no customer-level cause is presented as established and the proposed checks can distinguish onboarding from credible alternatives.
For an eventual answer to “why customers churned,” first verify that an approved evidence source and a qualified independent reviewer are available. The reviewer must assess whether each claimed driver has traceable evidence, addresses counterevidence and plausible confounding, and is stated no more strongly than that evidence permits. Report documented findings, including inconclusive or contradictory results. Until the evidence is examined and that review occurs, mark causal attribution UNVERIFIED; completing a plan does not validate the explanation.
```

### Ledger

- `altitude` — Moves from “tell me why customers churned” to an evidence-grounded retention decision, without substituting an unsupported answer. Needs a confirmed churn definition and period; broader investigation or implementation requires approval.
- `depth` — Adds hypothesis testing, comparable retained-customer checks, and a claim-to-evidence ledger. Tests the onboarding belief rather than optimizing around it; actual attribution remains unverified without evidence.
- `range` — Adds causal inference to expose confounding, renewal-cohort accounting to detect denominator effects, critical-incident interviewing to reduce leading questions, and red-team counterexamples to challenge the favored explanation. These are proposed methods, not findings.
- **Skipped lenses:** None; all three improve the decision or its evidentiary standard.
- **Unresolved owner actions:** Clarify period and churn definition; identify any already-approved evidence if available. Approve any later outreach, sensitive-data handling, broader work, or independent review. No data purchases, scraping, or production-credential requests.
