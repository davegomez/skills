### Rewritten prompt

```text
Help me understand why customers churned last month, distinguishing what is known from what would be needed to establish a cause.

Current evidence and constraints:
- I have no raw event logs, CRM export, or database access to share right now.
- All I know is that the sales team thinks onboarding is the cause.
- Do not buy data, scrape customer information, or request production credentials.
- Treat sales’ explanation as an unverified hypothesis, not a finding.

Assumption: I will use this answer to decide whether onboarding deserves retention investment. Help me avoid committing to the wrong explanation, without expanding this into a general retention project.

First establish the evidence boundary:
- Note that the calendar period, churn definition, affected population, and magnitude of churn are unspecified. Do not invent them or assume churn increased.
- Check whether any already-available, authorized evidence or qualified independent reviewer is available. Do not make the answer depend on receiving new data now.
- Any new access, customer contact, experiment, implementation, or work beyond this analysis requires my approval. The prohibitions above remain in force.

Produce a concise diagnostic brief:

1. What we can say now
State plainly whether the available information supports an explanation for last month’s churn. Separate observed information, reported beliefs, assumptions, and unknowns. Do not fabricate customer reasons, percentages, rankings, or causal conclusions.

2. Test the onboarding belief rather than repeat it
Use causal inference: sketch a small causal diagram in words showing how onboarding could affect churn, and how factors such as customer fit could affect both. Explain what evidence would distinguish an onboarding effect from correlation or selection bias.

Use reliability engineering’s competing-hypothesis method: compare onboarding with a few plausible alternatives. For each, name a distinguishing observation and an observation that would weaken it. These are proposed diagnostic signals, not observed findings.

Red-team the sales account: explain how the customers sales hears from, the timing of those conversations, and hindsight could bias the explanation. Do not assume sales is wrong.

3. Identify the smallest decisive next check
Specify the minimum evidence needed to test onboarding against its strongest alternative—not a broad analytics wishlist. Prefer existing, authorized, privacy-preserving evidence if it later becomes available. Name the raw source a strong analysis would use, but do not imply that you have accessed it.

Specify the comparison, churn definition, relevant time window, and key confounders before examining results. Explain what the comparison could and could not establish. A future experiment may test a retention intervention; it cannot by itself prove why customers churned last month.

Keep this as a proposal. Do not collect data or run an intervention without approval.

4. Decision now
Say what action, if any, the current evidence justifies, what should remain undecided, and what result would change that recommendation. If no cause can be established, make that limitation the answer rather than disguising hypotheses as findings.

Judge and observable pass condition:
First check whether a qualified independent analyst can review the brief; do not claim that review occurred unless it did. Have the reviewer check that every factual claim is traceable to supplied evidence, onboarding is not presented as established, alternatives have distinguishing tests, and the proposed next check can separate the leading explanations within its stated limitations. The brief passes only if the reviewer finds no unsupported factual or causal claims and accepts the next check as discriminating; otherwise revise it. Until that review runs, label the brief independently unverified. Any eventual explanation of last month’s churn must additionally be supported by authorized evidence covering that period; a proposed test or future intervention is not a verified historical finding.
```
