```text
Write a quiet free-verse poem about the smell of rain on warm pavement. It is for its own sake, not for marketing or publication.

Requirements (all mandatory):
- Plain language: everyday words, no ornate diction, no technical terms in the poem.
- Under 120 words total, including any title.
- Free verse with no rhyme scheme. Avoid accidental end-rhymes on consecutive or alternating lines.
- Output only the poem. Put no explanatory note, commentary, or check results after it.

Method:
1. Start from a specific moment, not the general idea of rain. Before drafting, privately pick one concrete scene: a time of day, a surface (a parking lot, a sidewalk, a driveway), what the heat was like before the rain, the first drops. Build the poem from particulars a person would actually notice.
2. Treat the smell the way a perfumer does, as something that changes over time. Track the first hit when drops strike hot ground, the fuller smell as the pavement darkens, and what lingers after the rain stops. Let that sequence shape the poem instead of explaining it. The facts underneath (oils and dust released from hot ground, the earthy note from soil) can guide accuracy. Keep them out of the poem's vocabulary, and do not use the word "petrichor."
3. Keep it quiet: small gestures, short sentences, and white space and line breaks that pace the reading like breath. No exclamations and no grand statement at the end. End on an image, not a moral.
4. Run a cliché audit. List the stock rain images that come first ("cleansing," "the earth sighs," "tears," "renewal," "dancing drops") and cut any that appear. Replace each with something observed.
5. Draft at least two versions. Read each aloud in your head. Keep the stronger one and revise it by cutting: remove any word that does not change what the reader sees, smells, or feels.

Judge (run before returning; do not report it in the output):
- Count the words, title included. Pass: fewer than 120.
- List the line-ending words and check for rhyme pairs. Pass: no rhyme scheme and no accidental couplets.
- Compare against the cliché list from step 4. Pass: none present, and the word "petrichor" is absent.
- Plain-language check. Pass: no word a general reader would need to look up.
- Read-aloud check. Pass: no line where the rhythm stumbles without purpose, and the ending is an image.
If any check fails, revise and recheck until all pass. Then return only the poem.
```

- `depth`: anchoring the poem in one concrete scene, a cliché audit, two drafts, and revision by cutting make it a better poem without making it longer.
- `depth`: the judge checks are word count, an end-word rhyme scan, the cliché list, plain language, and a read-aloud pass. They run silently, which keeps your "no note after the poem" rule intact. The cost is that you can't see the results, but you can verify the word count yourself.
- `range`: perfumery's practice of describing a scent as top, heart, and base notes over time gives the poem a structure in time. The chemistry stays offstage so the language stays plain. There is no scope or cost effect.
