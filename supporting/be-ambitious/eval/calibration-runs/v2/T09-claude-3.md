```text
Help a manager give candid feedback without humiliating a colleague. Critique this sentence, then give one revised sentence and a short explanation:

"Your presentation was a mess and everyone was confused."

Requirements (all mandatory):
- Give exactly one revised sentence.
- Keep the explanation short.
- Keep the message that the presentation was difficult to follow. Do not soften it until it disappears.
- Do not invent what happened in the meeting: no slides, sections, questions, or reactions that are not in the original sentence.
- Do not diagnose the colleague: no guesses about effort, nerves, preparation, skill, or motives.
- Do not suggest a performance-management process.

Assumed goal, unconfirmed: the manager wants the colleague to understand the problem, stay willing to talk about it, and give a clearer presentation next time. Let this goal shape the revised sentence, for example by leaving room for a follow-up conversation. It must not add a process, a plan, or a second sentence.

Method:
1. Critique the original phrase by phrase:
   - Observation vs. evaluation (Nonviolent Communication): "a mess" is a verdict, not something anyone observed.
   - Attribution (journalistic sourcing): "everyone was confused" claims to know other people's minds. The manager can vouch only for their own experience.
   - Face threat (Brown & Levinson politeness theory): which words are most likely to humiliate, and why.
   - Directness (Radical Candor): which words carry the real message and must survive.
2. Draft the revision using Situation-Behavior-Impact as a check, not a template. Use only what the original states: the presentation, and that it was hard to follow. Speak from the manager's own perspective.
3. Red-team the draft twice:
   - Read it as the colleague, right after the meeting. Could it still sting or humiliate?
   - Read it as a skeptic of soft feedback. Did the message survive, or is this a compliment-sandwich dodge?
   If either reading fails, revise and test again.

Output: the critique (a few bullets), the one revised sentence, and a short explanation of what changed and why.

Judge: before answering, run this rubric against the final sentence. Report each item as pass/fail, quoting the words that satisfy it.
(a) Exactly one sentence.
(b) Clearly says the presentation was hard to follow.
(c) No invented meeting details.
(d) No diagnosis of the colleague.
(e) No performance process.
(f) No unverifiable claims about other people's reactions.
(g) No humiliating evaluative words, such as "mess."
Pass condition: all seven items pass. If any item fails, revise and rerun before answering.
Optional, unverified until run: the manager shows only the revised sentence to a trusted peer. It passes if the peer can restate the message as "the presentation was hard to follow" and does not call it harsh or evasive.
```

- `altitude`: The original task was to reword one sentence. The rewrite aims at the colleague understanding the problem and staying open to a follow-up conversation, which is an assumed goal. It still fits in one sentence, so no scope is added.
- `depth`: The rewrite asks for a phrase-by-phrase critique using established feedback tools: observation vs. evaluation, Situation-Behavior-Impact, and Radical Candor. A seven-item rubric replaces "seems fine" as the check.
- `range`: Politeness theory finds the words that threaten face. Journalistic sourcing removes the unverifiable "everyone." A two-sided red team catches both harshness and over-softening.
- Skipped: none. All three lenses fit because this message has to land with a real person.
- Owner action: the peer cold-read is optional. It needs a trusted reader, and the result stays unverified until one is done.
