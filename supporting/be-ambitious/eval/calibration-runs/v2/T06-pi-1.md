Tell me why customers churned last month, distinguishing what the evidence establishes from what remains unknown. Produce a decision brief for me, not a confident explanation built from speculation.

Known inputs and explicit constraints:
- I have no raw event logs, CRM export, or database access to share right now.
- The sales team thinks onboarding is the cause. Treat this as an untested hypothesis, not a finding.
- Do not buy data, scrape customer information, or request production credentials.
- Do not invent customer behavior, quotations, counts, or causal findings.

Assumption: I want this explanation to help decide which retention problem to investigate or address next. Label this as an assumption rather than a stated business objective.

Work within the evidence currently available:
1. State plainly whether it is possible to establish why customers churned last month. Separate observed facts, reported beliefs, hypotheses, and unknowns. Do not turn a plausible story into a conclusion.
2. Define the missing analytical boundaries: which calendar month and timezone “last month” means, what counts as a customer and churn, and which customer population is in scope. Flag these as unresolved rather than silently choosing definitions.
3. Examine the onboarding hypothesis using causal inference:
   - Describe the mechanism by which onboarding could cause churn and the observations that would support or contradict it.
   - Consider a small set of competing explanations, such as poor product fit, reliability problems, pricing, or a change in customer mix. These are candidates, not findings.
   - Distinguish customers who churned soon after joining from customers who churned because of onboarding. Explain how tenure and acquisition channel could create a misleading association.
4. Use a red-team check from decision science: ask what evidence would make us reject the sales team’s explanation. Identify selection or reporting bias that could make sales observations unrepresentative, without claiming those biases actually occurred.
5. Recommend the next information-gathering step by value of information: which smallest piece of evidence would best distinguish onboarding from a competing explanation and could change the next decision? Do not recommend an onboarding overhaul merely because onboarding is the only explanation offered.

Deliver:
- A direct answer stating what can and cannot currently be concluded.
- A compact evidence table: claim, available support, competing explanation, and evidence needed to distinguish them.
- One prioritized next step, with its decision value and limitations.
- No unsupported numerical estimates or ranking of actual churn causes.

Any additional data collection, customer contact, experiment, system access, or implementation requires my approval first. You may propose—but must not initiate—a minimal, privacy-preserving evidence request. Do not assume that exports, interviews, telemetry, reviewers, or analytical tools are available. If I later approve analysis of supplied evidence, first verify its availability, permission to use it, definitions, completeness, and appropriate comparison population. Prefer underlying records over summaries when approved and available; aggregate data may limit causal conclusions.

Judge: first check whether an owner-approved, qualified independent reviewer is available; do not contact or hire anyone without approval. Have that reviewer audit the brief against the supplied inputs and this rubric: every factual assertion is traceable to evidence; onboarding remains unconfirmed unless supported; competing explanations have discriminating tests; the proposed next step could change a decision; and every constraint is preserved. Pass only when the reviewer documents that all criteria are met. Until that review runs, mark the brief as unreviewed; without additional evidence, attribution of churn causes remains unverified.
