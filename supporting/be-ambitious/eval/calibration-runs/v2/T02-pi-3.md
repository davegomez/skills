```text
Prepare the quarterly business review deck for our leadership meeting in two weeks. The deck is required, must contain at most 12 slides total, and must help leaders decide whether to invest in retention or acquisition.

Use existing staff and tools. Do not change our operating cadence or contact customers. Do not incur new costs, seek new access, or expand the work beyond this review without owner approval.

Intended result: leadership can make an evidence-backed allocation decision, understand the conditions under which it would change, and distinguish observed performance from projected returns.
Assumption: the goal is to improve the return on the next increment of investment, not simply maximize customer count or revenue. Confirm this objective and the relevant investment horizon with the owner; do not silently substitute your own.

1. Check dependencies first.
The owner can provide raw customer and revenue exports but has not shared them yet. Request those exports through an existing approved channel. Confirm available history, field definitions, reporting cutoff, currency, customer identifiers, and permitted handling of sensitive data. Use only the customer-level detail necessary; keep identifying information out of the deck.

Check whether existing sources also contain acquisition spending, retention-program costs, gross or contribution margins, channel attribution, and prior intervention results. These are dependencies, not assumed resources. Confirm the decision budget and financial success measure. If information is unavailable, identify what cannot be concluded and proceed with supported analysis. Do not invent data, access, or causal effects.

2. Build the decision from raw evidence.
Reconcile customer and revenue totals to the existing quarterly reporting baseline where available. Explain material discrepancies, exclusions, refunds, duplicate records, and incomplete periods.

Use cohort analysis to compare customers at equivalent ages rather than confuse recent growth with stronger retention. Separate new-customer revenue, expansion, contraction, and churn where the data supports it. Define customer retention and revenue retention separately. If using survival analysis to estimate retention, account for customers whose observation windows have not yet ended; avoid unsupported lifetime extrapolation.

Identify which customer segments or acquisition channels materially explain the result. Limit cuts to those that could change the decision, and label small samples and exploratory patterns.

3. Test the premise behind the choice.
Do not assume either acquisition or retention is inherently the better investment. Compare the next increment of each against the same current-plan baseline, time horizon, and budget constraint—not historical average performance alone.

Use contribution-margin economics, payback, and break-even analysis where supported. For retention, calculate how much incremental churn reduction or expansion would justify the spend. For acquisition, calculate the customer acquisition cost and subsequent retention needed to justify it. If costs or intervention effects are missing, present these as conditional thresholds, not measured returns.

Separate observed facts, modeled estimates, and hypotheses. Observational differences between groups are not proof that spending will cause the same differences. Use existing experiment results only after checking their relevance and validity.

4. Challenge the recommendation.
Use decision analysis: vary the assumptions most capable of reversing the choice, show plausible downside and upside cases, and identify the switching point between retention and acquisition.

Use a red-team check: make the strongest evidence-based case for the opposite recommendation, then test whether the preferred option still holds. Where adequate historical data exists, check key projections against a later period not used to construct them. Otherwise label predictive performance unverified.

Recommend retention or acquisition when the evidence supports a preference. If the choice remains sensitive to missing evidence, show a conditional recommendation and the exact unresolved input leadership must judge. Do not manufacture certainty.

5. Make the deck decision-ready.
Within 12 slides total, cover the decision requested, quarterly performance, cohort economics, comparable investment cases, uncertainties, recommendation, and conditions that would reverse it. Put the executive recommendation early. Give each analytical slide a takeaway, source, reporting window, and relevant caveat; use speaker notes for definitions and calculation traceability.

Work backward from the meeting in two weeks using existing working arrangements. Surface missing inputs promptly. If they remain unavailable, still deliver the required deck on time, clearly marked as provisional wherever evidence is missing. Any proposed follow-up must remain within existing cadence and resources; changes require owner approval, and customer contact remains prohibited.

Judge and observable pass condition:
Run reproducible checks using existing tools: customer/revenue reconciliations have no unexplained material differences; key figures and decision thresholds reproduce from the supplied inputs; cohort comparisons use equivalent observation windows; and sensitivity checks expose assumptions that reverse the recommendation. Report actual check results and unresolved failures in the notes. The deck passes when it is delivered within two weeks, contains no more than 12 slides, makes an explicit retention-versus-acquisition decision request with a supported or clearly conditional recommendation, and traces every material numerical claim to evidence or a labeled assumption. Missing evidence or checks not run remain explicitly unverified.
```
